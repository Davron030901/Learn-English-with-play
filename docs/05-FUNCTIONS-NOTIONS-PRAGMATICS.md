# 05 — FUNCTIONS, NOTIONS & PRAGMATICS
## What the learner needs to *do* with English, and how to do it appropriately

Grammar and vocabulary are the raw material. This document specifies the **communicative acts** the learner must perform, the **concepts** they must express, and the **social appropriacy** rules that decide whether a grammatically perfect sentence lands well or badly.

A learner who says *"Give me a coffee"* to a barista has made no grammatical error and a serious pragmatic one. Pragmatic failure is judged by listeners as *rudeness*, not as *learner error* — which makes it more socially costly than grammar failure and therefore a first-class part of this syllabus.

## 1. Functional syllabus (speech acts), by level

Each function is an ID (`F-###`) with: exponents ranked by formality, the level each exponent enters, prosody notes, and the typical response moves.

### 1.1 A1 — 42 functions

**Social ritual:** greeting · responding to greeting · introducing self · introducing others · leave-taking · thanking · responding to thanks · apologising · responding to apology · wishing well (birthday, weekend, journey)
**Information exchange:** asking/giving name, age, nationality, address, phone, email · asking/saying where something is · asking/telling the time · asking/saying the date · asking/giving prices · counting and quantifying · spelling aloud · confirming and denying
**Basic needs:** ordering food/drink · buying a ticket · asking for something (*Can I have…?*) · asking permission (*Can I…?*) · saying you don't understand · asking for repetition · asking for slower speech · asking how to say something in English · asking what a word means
**Expression:** saying you like/dislike · saying how you feel · agreeing/disagreeing simply · saying yes/no politely
**Directive:** giving simple instructions · giving simple directions · inviting (*Let's…*) · accepting/declining simply

### 1.2 A2 — +58 functions (100 total)

**Interaction management:** opening and closing a conversation · changing topic simply · checking understanding (*Do you mean…?*) · asking someone to repeat/clarify/spell · signalling you are thinking
**Transactional:** making a reservation · checking in/out · complaining simply · returning goods and asking for a refund · describing a problem (*It doesn't work*) · asking for help · reporting a loss · making a phone call, leaving a message
**Social:** making arrangements (time, place, confirmation) · inviting formally and informally · accepting/declining with a reason · making and responding to suggestions · offering and responding to offers · asking about and describing past events · telling a simple story · expressing sympathy · congratulating
**Opinion:** giving a simple opinion · agreeing/disagreeing with reasons · expressing preference · comparing
**Modality:** expressing ability, obligation, prohibition, permission, advice, possibility, intention, promise
**Description:** describing people, places, objects, routines, processes (simple)

### 1.3 B1 — +70 functions (170 total)

**Argument:** stating and supporting an opinion · giving reasons and examples · conceding a point · disagreeing politely · asking for someone's view · summarising a discussion · qualifying a statement
**Narrative:** telling an extended story with sequencing · reporting what someone said · describing a memory · speculating about the past
**Interpersonal:** apologising for something serious · making and responding to complaints · negotiating a simple compromise · refusing politely with mitigation · expressing enthusiasm/disappointment/regret/worry/relief · giving and receiving compliments · encouraging · warning · reassuring
**Transactional:** explaining a technical problem · giving detailed instructions · asking for and following directions in detail · dealing with a service failure · making a formal request in writing
**Discourse:** signalling a digression · returning to the topic · holding the floor · inviting someone to speak · interrupting politely · repairing a misunderstanding
**Speculation:** hypothesising · expressing regret about the past · giving advice about the past

### 1.4 B2 — +60 functions (230 total)

**Argumentation:** building a multi-step argument · anticipating and rebutting counter-arguments · distinguishing fact from opinion · evaluating evidence · weighing advantages and disadvantages · drawing conclusions · qualifying claims with hedges · conceding without surrendering (*Granted…, but*)
**Professional:** chairing a short meeting · presenting with signposting · handling hostile questions · negotiating terms · delegating · giving constructive criticism · receiving criticism · writing a report/proposal/formal complaint
**Interpersonal advanced:** softening bad news · declining an offer without offence · challenging an assertion respectfully · expressing scepticism · expressing strong emotion appropriately for register · defusing conflict · making small talk with strangers across cultures
**Discourse:** structuring extended monologue · using discourse markers for stance · reformulating (*What I mean is…*) · exemplifying · generalising and specifying · signposting text structure in writing
**Mediation:** summarising a source for someone else · relaying an argument neutrally · explaining a chart · translating gist

### 1.5 C1 — +50 functions (280 total)

Persuading and influencing · framing an issue · rhetorical questioning · irony and understatement · humour in interaction · flattery and its risks · face-saving and face-threatening act mitigation · establishing credibility · managing group dynamics · chairing a contentious discussion · giving a formal speech · toasting · condoling · interviewing and being interviewed · advocating for a position in writing · reviewing critically · abstracting and synthesising · academic hedging and boosting · citing and attributing · signalling disagreement with a source · mediating between conflicting parties · code-switching for audience

### 1.6 C2 — +40 functions (320 total)

Nuanced persuasion under adversarial conditions · calibrated ambiguity (saying enough and no more) · diplomatic non-answers · satire and parody · pastiche of register · rhetorical set-pieces (eulogy, keynote, apology in public) · negotiating across power asymmetry · testifying/formal statement · editing another's argument · reframing a debate · mediating cultural conflict · teaching/explaining expertly to a lay audience · improvising at length · repairing catastrophic misunderstanding · using silence and prosody as pragmatic tools

## 2. Exponent banks — worked example

Functions are useless without ranked exponents. Every function record looks like this.

### F-023 REQUESTING SOMETHING

| Exponent | Formality | Directness | Level | Notes |
|----------|-----------|------------|-------|-------|
| *Water.* | — | bare | — | Not taught. Rude in almost all contexts. |
| *Give me the salt.* | informal | very direct | A2 (receptive) | Only among intimates, with the right prosody. Flag as risky. |
| *Pass the salt, please.* | informal | direct | A1 | Fine at a family table. |
| *Can I have a coffee, please?* | neutral | conventionally indirect | A1 | The default service transaction. |
| *Could I have…?* | neutral+ | more indirect | A2 | Safer with strangers. |
| *Could you pass me…?* | neutral+ | hearer-oriented | A2 | |
| *Would you mind passing…?* | polite | indirect, + *-ing* | B1 | Answer trap: *No, not at all* = yes. Must be taught explicitly. |
| *Do you think you could…?* | polite | very indirect | B1 | |
| *I was wondering if you could possibly…* | very polite | maximally indirect | B2 | Past + hedge stacking; used for large impositions |
| *If it's not too much trouble, would you be able to…?* | very polite | | B2 | |
| *I'd be grateful if you could…* | formal, written | | B2 | Email register |
| *I should be most grateful if you would…* | very formal, written | | C1 | Officialese; note register risk |
| *Might I trouble you for…?* | formal/dated | | C1 | Receptive; flagged as marked |

**Politeness principle taught with it:** *the greater the imposition, the social distance, or the hearer's power, the more indirect the request.* This one rule generates correct behaviour across hundreds of situations and is taught explicitly at B1.

### F-058 DISAGREEING

| Exponent | Strength | Level |
|----------|----------|-------|
| *I don't think so.* | soft | A2 |
| *I'm not sure about that.* | soft | A2 |
| *I see what you mean, but…* | soft, concessive | B1 |
| *That's true up to a point, however…* | concessive | B1 |
| *I take your point, but…* | concessive | B2 |
| *I'm afraid I have to disagree.* | firm, polite | B2 |
| *I'm not convinced that…* | firm, hedged | B2 |
| *With respect, that's not quite right.* | firm, formal | B2 |
| *That's simply not the case.* | strong | C1 |
| *I couldn't disagree more.* | very strong | C1 |
| *You're wrong.* | blunt | — | Taught receptively with a strong warning |

**Cultural note taught at B1:** English-speaking professional norms strongly prefer the concession-first pattern (*Yes, but…*). Direct contradiction that is normal and friendly in some cultures reads as aggressive in English-language workplaces. This is taught as *strategy*, not as a claim that one culture is better.

## 4. Discourse competence

| Feature | Content | Level |
|---------|---------|-------|
| **Turn-taking** | Recognising transition-relevance places (falling intonation + syntactic completion + gaze); taking a turn; yielding; holding | B1→B2 |
| **Openings & closings** | Greeting sequences, pre-closings (*Anyway…, Right, so…, I'd better let you go*), closing sequences | A2→B1 |
| **Adjacency pairs** | question–answer, offer–accept/decline, invitation–accept/decline, compliment–response, apology–minimisation, greeting–greeting | A1→B1 |
| **Preferred vs dispreferred responses** | Dispreferred (refusals, disagreements) require delay, hesitation, account, mitigation. *"Um… well, the thing is…"* is a **grammatical** signal of a coming refusal. Taught explicitly at B1 — misunderstanding this makes learners seem blunt. | B1 |
| **Repair** | Self-initiated self-repair; other-initiated repair (*Sorry? Pardon? What do you mean?*); clarification requests; confirmation checks; comprehension checks | A2→B2 |
| **Backchannelling** | *mm, right, yeah, uh-huh, I see, really?, wow* — culturally calibrated; under-use makes a listener seem cold, over-use seems impatient | B1 |
| **Topic management** | Introducing, shifting, returning, closing a topic; digression markers | B1→B2 |
| **Floor-holding** | Fillers (*well, you know, I mean, sort of, let me think*), level intonation, non-final rise, syntactic incompletion | B2 |
| **Cohesion devices** | Reference, substitution, ellipsis, conjunction, lexical cohesion | A2→C1 |
| **Coherence & genre structure** | Problem–solution, claim–evidence, chronological, compare–contrast, cause–effect patterns; genre moves | B1→C1 |
| **Information structure** | Given/new, theme/rheme, end-focus, end-weight | B2→C1 |
| **Written text organisation** | Paragraphing, topic sentences, transitions, introductions and conclusions per genre | B1→C2 |

## 5. Sociolinguistic competence

### 5.1 Register scale (taught explicitly from B1 as a five-point scale)

| Register | Marker features | Example of "I can't come" |
|----------|-----------------|---------------------------|
| **Frozen/ceremonial** | fixed formulae, archaism | *I regret that I shall be unable to attend.* |
| **Formal** | Latinate lexis, full forms, passive, nominalisation, no contractions | *Unfortunately I will not be able to attend.* |
| **Neutral/consultative** | standard, mixed | *I'm afraid I can't make it.* |
| **Informal** | phrasal verbs, contractions, ellipsis, discourse markers | *Sorry, I can't make it.* |
| **Intimate/slang** | in-group lexis, heavy ellipsis | *Can't make it, sorry!* |

**Core B1–B2 exercise type:** the same message rendered at all five registers; the learner matches register to situation, then produces.

### 5.2 Dimensions that determine register
power (up/down/equal) · social distance (intimate/familiar/stranger) · size of imposition · medium (spoken/written/text/email/formal letter) · setting (private/public/institutional) · purpose (transactional/relational) · culture and national norms.

### 5.3 Politeness (taught as strategy, B1→C1)
- **Face**: positive face (want to be liked) vs negative face (want to be unimpeded).
- **Face-threatening acts**: requests, refusals, disagreements, criticisms, interruptions, complaints.
- **Mitigation toolkit**: hedges (*a bit, kind of, maybe*), modal past (*I wanted to ask*), impersonalisation (*it seems that*), passive, question form, apology preface, giving a reason, offering an alternative, minimising (*just a quick question*), pre-sequences (*Are you busy?*).
- **Positive-politeness toolkit**: naming, in-group markers, compliments, agreement-seeking, small talk, humour, inclusive *we*.
- **The "not X but Y" softening pattern**, the **sandwich** pattern for criticism, and the **"I" statement** pattern for conflict.

### 5.4 Intercultural competence (B2→C2)
- Small talk topics: safe (weather, travel, food, sport, work) vs risky (salary, age, weight, religion, politics, marital status) — and how this varies between US/UK/AUS and between English-speaking contexts globally.
- Directness gradients: US/UK indirect request norms vs Dutch/German/Russian/Uzbek directness norms; neither is "polite" or "rude" in the abstract.
- Compliment responses: deflection vs acceptance.
- Silence tolerance; overlap vs latching norms.
- Humour: irony and self-deprecation in British English; enthusiasm norms in American English.
- Email openings/closings by culture and industry.
- **English as a Lingua Franca**: most English conversations in the world have no native speaker in them. From B2, learners practise accommodation strategies with other L2 speakers, not only with natives. This is a deliberate departure from native-speaker-idealising syllabi and matches actual use.

## 7. Mediation (CEFR 2020 Companion Volume) — a first-class strand

Mediation is under-taught everywhere and is half of real-world English use. It appears from A1 and becomes dominant at C1–C2.

| Mediation activity | A1–A2 | B1–B2 | C1–C2 |
|--------------------|-------|-------|-------|
| **Relaying specific information** | Pass on a time, price, name from a notice | Relay details from a call or email | Relay complex specifications accurately |
| **Explaining data** | Read a simple price list aloud | Describe a chart or graph | Interpret complex data sets and qualify conclusions |
| **Processing text** | Say what a short notice means | Summarise an article in 100 words; note-take from a talk | Synthesise multiple conflicting sources; abstract a research paper |
| **Translating** | Give the gist of an L1 sign | Translate the sense of an L1 message | Produce a faithful, register-appropriate translation |
| **Note-taking** | Copy key details | Take notes from a lecture | Take structured notes from fast, unstructured speech |
| **Expressing reaction to creative text** | *I like it because…* | Describe the theme and one's response | Critique style, structure and effect |
| **Facilitating interaction** | Introduce two people | Manage a pair discussion; check everyone understands | Chair a meeting; manage conflict; ensure equitable participation |
| **Collaborating on meaning** | Agree on a simple plan | Build on others' ideas; ask clarifying questions | Reformulate group thinking; synthesise dissent |
| **Managing plurilingual repertoire** | Use L1 to help understanding | Switch strategically | Mediate across cultures and languages |

**Product implication:** the app must contain mediation task types — summarise-this, explain-this-chart, relay-this-message, note-take-this-talk, reconcile-these-two-accounts — from B1. These are also the tasks where an LLM grader is genuinely useful, because there is no single correct answer but there are clear criteria.
