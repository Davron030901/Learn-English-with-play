# 03 — GRAMMAR SYLLABUS
## Complete structural inventory, A1 → C2, with sequencing rules and teaching notes

**Total: 412 grammar points.** Every point has an immutable ID (`G-###`), a level, prerequisites, a form/meaning/use triple, a known-error list, and at least 6 exercise types bound to it.

---

## 1. Sequencing principles (why the order is what it is)

1. **Frequency first.** The structures that occur most often in a 2,000-word conversation are taught first, regardless of "simplicity". *There is/are* is structurally odd but indispensable at A1.
2. **Communicative payoff.** A structure is taught when it unlocks a function the learner needs *now* (see doc 05).
3. **Teachability / natural order.** Some morphemes are acquired in a fixed order regardless of instruction (Krashen/Pienemann): *-ing* → plural *-s* → copula *be* → auxiliaries → articles → irregular past → regular past *-ed* → 3rd person *-s* → possessive *-'s*. The syllabus **introduces** in communicative order but **expects mastery** in this natural order; the SRS must not punish a learner for late 3rd-person -s, which is developmentally last.
4. **Contrast late.** Two structures that contrast (present perfect vs past simple; *make/do*; *some/any*) are introduced *separately and apart*, then contrasted deliberately in a dedicated "clinic" lesson. Introducing contrasts together doubles the error rate.
5. **Form before contrast, meaning before both.** A structure is met first as meaning-in-context (a story, a dialogue), then noticed, then practised, then contrasted.
6. **Spiral.** Every structure returns at least three times: introduction, expansion, and register/nuance. E.g. the passive: form (B1.1) → agentless/reason for use (B1.2) → reporting passives and nominalisation (B2.2) → stylistic choice (C1).
7. **Chunk before rule.** *How are you? / I'd like… / Would you like…?* enter at A1 as unanalysed chunks (`chunk: true`) and are re-analysed grammatically much later.

## 11. How each grammar point is taught (the mandatory 7-step cycle)

1. **Encounter in text** — the structure appears in a story/dialogue, unglossed. The learner meets it as meaning.
2. **Noticing task** — the learner is asked to find or highlight instances. (Not explained yet.)
3. **Guided discovery** — 3–4 examples plus a question the learner answers to derive the rule themselves ("Which sentence is about a finished time?"). Rule is then confirmed in ≤ 40 words plus a diagram.
4. **Controlled practice** — recognition, then gap-fill, then transformation. Feedback immediate.
5. **Contrast** (if applicable) — the clinic.
6. **Freer production** — a task that *forces* the structure (e.g. "Tell me three things you have never done") — Swain's pushed output.
7. **Spaced recall** — the point enters the SRS as (a) a form item, (b) a meaning-choice item, (c) a production item. All three must reach stability before the point counts as mastered.

**Grammar explanations must obey:** ≤ 40 words; one diagram/timeline; one correct and one incorrect example; no metalanguage the learner has not been taught. A learner should never have to learn grammar terminology in order to learn grammar — but the terms are available on tap in a glossary, because some learners want them.
